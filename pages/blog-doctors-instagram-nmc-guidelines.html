Doctors Who Post on Instagram: Please Stop Breaking These NMC Guidelines

Instagram can be a useful public-health education channel. It can also create professional-conduct risk when a post turns into self-promotion, a patient acquisition campaign or an implied promise of results. The question is not whether you are allowed to post; it is whether each post is accurate, transparent, respectful of patient privacy and consistent with the current rules that apply to you.

A practical rule

Ask: “Would this still be useful if the viewer never booked with me?” If the answer is no, review the post carefully. Do not assume that a disclaimer or a small caption can cure an otherwise promotional or misleading post.

You may be breaking NMC guidelines by not doing these things

1. Put your name and qualifications upfront

Disclose your name and recognised qualifications clearly. Best practice is to say or display them in the video itself. If that is not feasible, put the disclosure prominently in every caption—not buried among hashtags or hidden behind “more.” Do not claim a specialist title, award or credential you cannot substantiate, and include registration particulars where required by the applicable professional rules.

2. Do not post patient testimonials

Do not publish patient testimonials, before-and-after stories, “transformation” posts, cure claims or case material designed to showcase your outcomes. Even where a patient has agreed, a testimonial can function as publicity or solicitation and may expose private health information. Never use a patient’s image, report or story to imply that similar results are guaranteed.

3. Add a “not medical advice” disclaimer

State clearly that the content is general education and not medical advice. Best practice is to say this in the video itself; if that is not feasible, include it in every caption. Also tell viewers not to self-diagnose, not to share sensitive reports in comments, and when to seek urgent or in-person care. A disclaimer supports clarity but does not authorise individualised diagnosis or treatment in public comments.

4. Keep the post educational, not an advertisement

Avoid exaggerated claims, guaranteed outcomes, fear-based hooks, “best doctor” language, inducements, paid patient acquisition and content that invites attention to your skill or success as a sales pitch. Organic public-health education is different from paying to boost a Reel or using a third party to procure patients.

5. Make the next step transparent

If you have an Online OPD, explain what it is, what teleconsultation can and cannot do, the fee, consent process and how patients can access in-person care when needed. Keep a neutral “find another doctor near you” option visible. A clear patient journey is more trustworthy than a hidden funnel.

A quick pre-publish check

Credentials visible? No testimonial or transformation? Disclaimer included? No diagnosis in comments? No guaranteed result or paid solicitation? Patient privacy protected? If any answer is no, edit the post or do not publish it. Ask your website or social-media team to use this checklist too—but retain final responsibility for professional content published under your name.
